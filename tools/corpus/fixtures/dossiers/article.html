Larkspur Tokamak

TypeTokamak
Operated1987 to 2004

The Larkspur Tokamak was an experimental magnetic confinement device built at the invented Larkspur Plasma Laboratory in 1987.[1] It held a deuterium plasma for a record 4.2 seconds in its third season, which the laboratory announced at a crowded press briefing.[2]

Engineers on the project described the vacuum vessel as a doughnut made of stubborn steel, and the phrase stayed in the laboratory's newsletters for a decade.[citation needed]

History[edit]

Construction began in the spring of 1984 after a long argument about the size of the central solenoid. The first coil was wound by hand over eleven weeks, and the team kept a tally of every turn on a chalkboard by the door. The second coil took only six weeks because the winders had learned to pre-tension the copper strip before each pass. Inspectors from the regional energy office visited twice during the winding and signed off on both coils without changes. A flood in the basement delayed the cryogenic plant by four months in the winter of 1985. The pumps were replaced with sealed units that could run under a metre of water, a precaution nobody needed again. First plasma arrived on a Tuesday evening in March 1987, lasting nineteen milliseconds before a disruption ended it. The control room log records that the operators cheered and then spent the night checking the first wall for damage. Over the following year the pulse length grew steadily as the feedback system learned to hold the plasma column in place. By the autumn of 1988 the device was routinely holding plasmas for more than a second at modest currents. The laboratory published its pulse records every quarter in a slim grey report that was mailed to other fusion groups around the world. Those reports are now the main source for the device's operating history, because the original control software was lost in a later office move. A retired technician later rebuilt part of the timing system from printed listings found in a filing cabinet. His notes describe a clock board that drifted by a few microseconds every hour and had to be reset each morning before the first shot. Visitors were allowed into the hall on open days, when the device was cold and the cryostat doors stood open.

Funding ended in 2004 and the device was decommissioned over the following two years, with its coils donated to a teaching laboratory.[3]

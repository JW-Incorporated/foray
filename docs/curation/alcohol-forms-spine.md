# Spine — all the forms of alcohol (`alcohol-forms-1`)

The canonical narrative, written **source-blind**. Nothing in `data/` was read to
produce this: not the segment pool, not `segments.json`, not
`segment-sources.json`, not `discover.json`, not `forays.json`, not an ASR
manifest, not a transcript, not the catalogue search. Only #226 and its founder
comment, `docs/curation/grilling-history-spine.md` as a format precedent, and
`docs/curation/segment-length-rules.md`.

That constraint is the point, and it is worth restating in the document itself so
nobody later "improves" the spine by checking it against the tape. The previous
Foray drifted into Welsh bakestones, medieval manners and 18th-century dinner
parties because its outline was assembled from the tape that happened to exist.
An outliner who can see the catalogue writes an outline the catalogue fits, and
after that no downstream filter can recover the plot, because the plot has
already been bent. So the spine is written first and blind, and the tape is
scored against it.

**The request, verbatim from the founder:** "Build a foray for me that educates me
about all the forms of alcohol. What are all of them, walk me through the
production process for each, maybe a touch of history."

Three words in that decide the shape of this document, and they are in priority
order. **"All of them"** — the taxonomy has to be complete enough that a curious
listener can name a drink and find it. **"Production process"** — the mechanism
is the body of the work, not colour around a story. **"A touch of history"** —
seasoning, explicitly. This is the opposite weighting from the barbecue Foray,
which was history-led with technique in service of it. Here technique leads and
history serves. §2c states the rule that keeps history in its place, because
history is where this subject will drift if anything does.

**Status:** proposed. Stage 1 of the four stages in #226. Stage 2 sources each
beat independently against that beat's claim; stage 3 reports coverage; stage 4
assembles in spine order under `segment-length-rules.md`.

**This spine is expected to have holes.** A beat with no tape is a narration
beat, not a beat to be deleted or substituted for. §5 lists the beats I predict
will be hardest to source — as a *prediction for the coverage report*, not a
filter. Nothing was dropped, shortened or softened on a guess about
availability, and guessing at availability is the contamination this document
exists to prevent.

## 1. The through-line

"All the forms of alcohol" is a shopping list. Here is the argument that turns it
into a Foray.

> **Every alcoholic drink on earth is the same molecule, made the same way, by
> the same organism: yeast eating sugar. The thousands of drinks are not
> thousands of inventions — they are the answers to four questions asked in
> order, and the whole taxonomy is a decision tree, not a catalogue. Where did
> the sugar come from, and did it have to be unlocked first? How far was the
> yeast allowed to go? Was the result concentrated? And what was added or done to
> it while it waited? Learn to ask those four in that order and you can place a
> drink you have never heard of, which is what "educates me" actually asks for.**

The four questions, stated once here because the entire document is their
expansion:

1. **Where is the sugar, and is it free or locked?** Free in grapes, honey, cane,
   apples, palm sap, milk. Locked in the starch of barley, rice, maize, sorghum,
   cassava, potato — and locked differently again in agave, whose store is
   fructan rather than starch. This question alone splits the fermented world in
   two, and Acts II and III are its two halves.
2. **What converts it, and how far can it go?** Yeast, mostly one domesticated
   species, which makes a poison it eventually cannot survive. That gives a hard
   ceiling near 14 to 16 % ABV, and that number is the load-bearing constant of
   the subject: it is why every drink above about 16 % had to be either
   concentrated or fortified.
3. **Was it concentrated?** Distillation is the second operation, it separates
   rather than creates, and it is the fork between the fermented drinks and the
   spirits. It is also historically recent — most of the world's drinking is
   thousands of years old, and most of the world's spirits are a few hundred.
4. **What happened afterwards?** Wood and time, which is a chemistry of
   extraction, oxidation and loss. Or addition — spirit, sugar, botanicals —
   which is a category of drinks defined by what was put in rather than by what
   fermented.

Two consequences of writing it this way, both deliberate.

**The listener is being armed, not toured.** The test of Act I is whether a
listener who has finished it can predict what a drink will be like from its
feedstock and its still. If they can, every family afterwards is a variation they
reason about themselves, which is the difference between education and a list.
That is why Act I gets a quarter of the runtime despite containing no drinks.

**Completeness is a structural claim, not a courtesy.** "All of them" is the
founder's word and it earns the outliers a place: pulque, palm wine, kumis,
chicha, huangjiu, baijiu, shochu, cachaça, arrack, feni. Several get a full beat
because they are *process* outliers — they do something no other family does — and
several are named inside a neighbouring beat. §6c lists which and why. What is
not defensible is a spine that covers eight famous drinks and calls it the world.

The arc the beats follow:

**one molecule → the sugar problem → the ceiling → concentration → wood and time
→ the families that only need a ferment → the families that need a conversion →
the families that need a still → the families defined by addition → the rules
that were written down**

Two tests this through-line has to keep passing, because they are what the old
outline failed:

- **The relevance test is the beat, not the topic.** A segment is not admitted
  for being about drink, or booze, or history, or a distillery. It is admitted
  for advancing the claim of the beat it is assigned to (#226, founder rule 4).
- **Length is an output.** The proportions in §4 are pacing guidance against a
  150-minute reference. They are not quotas, and a beat that comes in at half its
  share because that is all the good tape there was has not failed.

## 2. Where the story chains, and where it fans

### 2a. The structure is a fan of chains, and that is the inverse of the barbecue spine

The barbecue spine had a flat fan in the middle: eight world traditions, none
descended from another, any of which could go missing at the cost of one stop on
a tour. This subject is shaped differently and the difference matters to
assembly.

**The families are a fan. The inside of each family is a chain.** Wine, beer,
sake, rum and sherry do not descend from one another — a listener who never hears
the mead beat has missed a stop, not a link. But *inside* the beer family the
beats are malt, then mash, then hops, then yeast, in that order, because that is
the order the process happens in. Drop the mash beat and the listener is left
holding an explanation with a hole in the middle of it, which is much worse than
a missing family.

So the coverage report has three grades of hole, not two:

| kind of hole | reads as | cost |
|---|---|---|
| a missing beat inside Act I | a broken explanation | **highest** — Act I is one causal chain and it is the education the founder asked for |
| a missing step inside a family | the process narrated up to a gap and then resumed past it | **high** — narrate it |
| a missing whole family stop | a change of subject | **low**, if narration announces the family boundary |

That table is the actionable output of this section. **Act I is the most
dangerous place in the spine for a hole and the small family stops in Acts II
through V are the safest.**

### 2b. Act by act

| Act | beats | structure | what the listener should feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — One molecule, four questions | 1–16 | **chain**, causal | each beat is required by the one before; nothing here is skippable |
| II — Sugar that was already sweet | 17–25 | **fan** of families, **chain** inside wine (17–21) | the first question answered the easy way |
| III — Sugar that had to be unlocked | 26–35 | **fan** of families, **chain** inside beer (26–31) and koji (32–33) | the same physics, one extra step, and most of the world's drinking |
| IV — Concentration | 36–51 | beat 36 is a **chain** link; 37–51 a **fan** by feedstock, with a **chain** inside whisky (37–40) | one machine, applied to everything already met |
| V — Made by addition | 52–60 | beat 52 is a **chain** link; 53–56 a **chain** by *when* the spirit goes in; 57–60 a **fan** | the categories that are not a fermentation at all |
| VI — The rules, and the coda | 61–63 | **chain**, closing | what the listener now holds, and how much of it is statute rather than chemistry |

Four notes on the seams, all of which are assembly instructions as much as
editorial ones.

- **Act I cannot be reordered and cannot be entered late.** It is a derivation:
  question 2 is unintelligible before question 1, distillation is unintelligible
  before the ceiling, and the classification key at beat 16 is unintelligible
  before all of it. If a beat in Act I comes back empty, narrate it. Do not skip
  it and do not move it, and above all do not compensate by lengthening a
  neighbour — Act I's job is a derivation, and a derivation with a longer step in
  place of a missing one is still broken.
- **Beat 16 is the hinge and it is the beat the whole Foray is for.** It hands the
  listener the four questions as a working key and explicitly walks one unfamiliar
  drink through it. Narration entering beat 17 should say plainly that everything
  from here is the key applied, and that the families do not descend from each
  other. Then a missing family costs the listener nothing.
- **Each family boundary must be announced as one.** This is the fan-signalling
  rule from the barbecue spine, applied more often because there are more fans. An
  unannounced move from mead to sap wines reads as the narrative losing its place;
  announced, it reads as the next branch of the key.
- **Acts IV and V are ordered by process, not by prestige or geography.** Act IV
  runs feedstock by feedstock in the order Acts II and III established — grain
  first because Act III just left the listener holding malting and koji, then
  cane, then fruit, then agave, then the rest — so that every spirit arrives as
  "the thing you already met, concentrated." A listener will try to infer a
  principle from the order and will be misled by an arbitrary one. Do not reorder
  Act IV into a tour of famous brown spirits.

### 2c. Where history goes, and the rule that keeps it there

**History is not an act. It is admitted beat by beat, and only where it explains
a production fact.** The test is a sentence you have to be able to attach
truthfully:

> *…and that is why the process looks like this.*

A history claim that passes that test is load-bearing seasoning and belongs
inside the process beat it explains. A history claim that fails it is a different
Foray. The places where the history genuinely *is* the mechanism, and where it
therefore lives:

- the alchemists' alembic, and what distillation was originally *for* — beat 36;
- the continuous column still, 1813 to 1831, which is the single most
  consequential machine in the subject — beat 12;
- pure yeast culture and artificial refrigeration, which is why lager could leave
  Bavaria — beats 4 and 28;
- phylloxera, which is why nearly every vine on earth is now a graft — beat 21;
- the shipping problems that invented brandy, port and madeira — beats 45, 53, 55;
- the post-war Korean rice ban, which is why mass-market soju is not distilled
  from rice — beat 48;
- the statutes that define whole categories, which are production specifications
  wearing legal clothing — beat 61;
- the tax hydrometer, which is why so much of the world's spirit sits at 40 % —
  beat 62.

That is the touch of history, and it is about eight per cent of the runtime
distributed across process beats rather than gathered into an act. Gathering it
into an act is precisely what would let it grow, because the tape for it is
abundant and the tape for mash-tun chemistry is not.

**The named drift risk.** In this subject the Welsh bakestone is **prohibition and
temperance**. It is dramatic, heavily covered, adjacent to every beat, and it
explains almost no production fact. It is not a beat. It is named in §6b so the
boundary is written down, and the only prohibition-shaped material that may enter
this Foray is material that explains a *process* — the Korean rice ban at beat 48,
bottled-in-bond at beat 61, the collapse and re-composition of specific
categories at beat 61 — and it enters those beats as evidence, not as its own
subject.

The second drift risk is **cocktails**, for the same reason: abundant, adjacent,
and about assembly in a glass rather than about how any of it was made.

## 3. The beats

**63 beats.** §4a derives that number rather than choosing it. Each has four
fields.

- **Claim** — the specific thing this beat asserts. This is what tape is scored
  against, and it is deliberately a sentence that could be true or false, not a
  topic label. "Fermentation" is not a claim. "Ethanol is a yeast's metabolic
  waste and the ceiling is where it poisons itself" is.
- **Why it belongs** — what the narrative loses if the beat is cut. Beats whose
  answer was weak are in §6a, dropped. §6b lists whole subjects excluded, which is
  a different question, and §6c records what is named inside a beat rather than
  given one.
- **Evidence that counts** — what a good 75–180 s piece of tape actually
  contains, concrete enough to score a candidate against. Where a name appears it
  is an example of the *kind* of speaker, not a requirement, and not a claim that
  such tape exists — I cannot know that.
- **Share** — proportion of runtime, with minutes at a 150-minute reference.
  Pacing guidance. Not a quota, and never a reason to admit weak tape.

Shares sum to 100 %. A beat's share is a statement about how much the *argument*
needs, decided before anyone knew what was available.

**One editorial instruction that applies to every beat in the document.** Several
of this subject's best-loved origin stories are undocumented, disputed, or
demonstrably marketing: the monk who invented sparkling wine, the goatherd who
discovered coffee's cousin, the ship that invented madeira, absinthe's madness,
the etymology of "proof", the single inventor of distillation. Where a beat rests
on one of these, the beat says so, and the correct register is **"this is the
tradition's own account"** rather than assertion. A listener who knows the subject
will hear a wrong claim immediately, and getting the chemistry right and the
folklore wrong would forfeit the credibility Act I spends its runtime earning.

### Act I — One molecule, four questions (chain, 26.6 %)

The act that earns the word "educates" in the founder's request. It contains no
drinks and it is a quarter of the Foray, and both of those are deliberate: this is
where the listener is given the apparatus to reason with, and every act after it
is that apparatus being exercised. It is a strict causal chain — see §2a — and it
ends pointed at the fan, which is what beat 16 is for.

#### 1. There are many alcohols and we drink exactly one of them, for a reason

**Claim.** "Alcohol" in chemistry is a whole family of compounds, we drink only
ethanol, and the reason is metabolic rather than cultural: the enzymes that break
down ethanol turn methanol into formic acid and the longer alcohols into worse
things, so ethanol is the one our biochemistry happens to tolerate at recreational
doses — and every drink in this Foray is essentially the same ethanol and water,
differing in a fraction of a percent of everything else.

**Why it belongs.** It is the beat that makes the whole spine's promise credible:
if the active ingredient is identical everywhere, then the entire subject *is*
production, which is exactly what the founder asked to be walked through. It also
sets up the two facts the Foray keeps needing — that flavour lives in the trace
compounds rather than in the ethanol, and that methanol is a real hazard with a
specific mechanism, which beat 13 collects on.

**Evidence that counts.** A chemist, food scientist or distiller stating the
distinction and the numbers: the hydroxyl group and what makes an alcohol an
alcohol, ethanol versus methanol versus isopropanol, alcohol dehydrogenase acting
on both, formaldehyde and formic acid as the reason methanol blinds and kills. The
strongest version also states the proportion plainly — that a spirit is
approximately 40 % ethanol, 60 % water and a fraction of a percent of congeners,
and that the fraction is where all the argument is. Reject: tasting-note
vocabulary, and "alcohol is a depressant" pharmacology, which is §6b.

**Share.** 1.6 % (~2.4 min).

#### 2. Fermentation is a yeast's energy metabolism, and we are drinking its waste

**Claim.** One molecule of glucose becomes two of ethanol and two of carbon
dioxide — the yeast is extracting energy without needing oxygen, ethanol is the
dead end it excretes, and the reaction has a fixed theoretical yield of about
51 % of the sugar's weight as ethanol, of which real ferments deliver roughly 48 %
because some sugar goes to yeast biomass and glycerol instead.

**Why it belongs.** This is the shared chemistry the whole through-line rests on,
and stating it as an equation rather than as a mood is what lets every later beat
be quantitative. Give the listener this and they can do the arithmetic themselves
for the rest of the Foray: sugar in, alcohol out, at a known ratio, which is why
grape ripeness sets wine strength and why a brewer measures gravity. Cut it and
Act I has no foundation and beat 5's ceiling has nothing to be a ceiling on.

**Evidence that counts.** A brewer, winemaker, biochemist or fermentation
scientist working through the pathway out loud: glycolysis, pyruvate,
decarboxylation to acetaldehyde, reduction to ethanol, and the CO2 accounting.
Strong signal: someone who corrects the common simplification that fermentation
only happens without oxygen — a well-fed yeast ferments in air as well, and needs a
little oxygen early to build its own cell membranes, so "anaerobic" is a
convenience rather than a rule. Also strong: gravity or Brix or Plato used as a
working instrument, with the conversion to expected ABV done aloud. Reject:
"yeast turns sugar into alcohol" with no mechanism and no numbers — that is a
label, not this beat.

**Share.** 2.0 % (~3.0 min).

#### 3. The other product is a gas, and every drink is a decision about whether to keep it

**Claim.** Fermentation produces as much carbon dioxide as ethanol, so every
producer has to decide whether to vent it or trap it — and if they trap it, they
must do so in a vessel that can hold five or six atmospheres, which is why
sparkling drinks are a late technology dependent on strong glass and a cork, and
why the world's fizzy drinks are all made by one of only three moves: ferment in a
sealed container, ferment a second time in a sealed container, or inject the gas
afterwards.

**Why it belongs.** It converts fizz from a property of certain drinks into a
single mechanism the listener can apply across families — champagne, cider, beer,
sparkling sake, prosecco, hard seltzer — and it pre-loads beat 20 so that champagne
arrives as an engineering problem rather than as a monk's miracle. Cut it and
carbonation has to be re-explained separately in four later beats.

**Evidence that counts.** A winemaker, brewer or cider maker on pressure and
containment: the atmospheres involved, bottle strength and why exploding bottles
were the historical constraint, the difference between conditioning in the vessel,
a second fermentation, and forced carbonation, and what dissolved CO2 does to
perceived acidity and aroma delivery. Strong signal: an explicit statement that
the traditional attribution of sparkling wine to a single 17th-century monk is a
house story, and that stronger English glass and reliable corks are the actual
enabling technologies. Reject: champagne romance, and any tape whose CO2 content
is a service story about pouring.

**Share.** 1.3 % (~1.95 min).

#### 4. Yeast is a domesticated organism, and choosing a strain is a production decision

**Claim.** Almost all the world's alcohol is made by *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*
and its relatives, humans have selected it for millennia without knowing it
existed, and the moment it could be isolated and grown pure — Pasteur establishing
that fermentation is a living process in the 1860s and 1870s, Hansen isolating a
single lager strain at Carlsberg in 1883 — production changed from something that
happened to something that could be repeated.

**Why it belongs.** It answers the question a listener will otherwise carry
through the whole Foray: if it is all the same reaction, why does it taste
different? Because the organism is a variable, and because before 1883 it was an
uncontrolled one. It is also the first of the three genuine history-is-mechanism
beats in Act I — the ancestor of every consistent industrial drink is a pure
culture — and it sets up beat 10, beat 28 and beat 30, which is the branch of
brewing that deliberately gives the control back.

**Evidence that counts.** A yeast scientist, brewer or historian of science on
domestication and pure culture: what selection did to the organism, why wild
ferment is variable, what Pasteur actually established as against what he is
credited with, and what Hansen's isolation made possible commercially. Strong
signal: naming the distinct species and what each is for — *cerevisiae* for ale,
wine and most spirits; *pastorianus* for lager and the fact that it is a hybrid
rather than a variety; *Brettanomyces* as the one deliberately kept for its
phenols; the lactose-fermenting yeasts that beat 25 needs. Reject: brewery-tour
yeast anecdote with no genetics and no consequence.

**Share.** 1.5 % (~2.25 min).

#### 5. Yeast poisons itself, and the number where it stops is the hinge of the whole subject

**Claim.** Ethanol is toxic to the organism producing it, so most fermentations
stall somewhere between 14 and 16 % ABV whatever sugar remains — which means every
drink stronger than that on earth got there by one of exactly three routes:
distillation, fortification with spirit, or a specialised high-tolerance process,
of which sake's staged fermentation reaching about 18 to 20 % before dilution is
the world's most successful example.

**Why it belongs.** This is the load-bearing constant of the through-line and the
single most useful fact in the Foray. It is what makes the taxonomy a decision
tree rather than a list: the fermented families all live below the ceiling, the
spirits all live above it because a machine put them there, and the fortified
wines are the third case where the machine's output was poured into the first
case. Cut this and Act IV is a new topic instead of a consequence, and the
listener has no way to know why anyone bothered inventing a still.

**Evidence that counts.** A fermentation scientist, brewer or sake producer
explaining ethanol tolerance mechanically — membrane stress, why some strains
tolerate more, why stuck fermentations happen and what a producer does about them —
and stating the ceiling as a number. Strong signal: someone enumerating the ways
around it, and getting sake right as an unusually high fermentation rather than
as a fortified or distilled drink. Also strong: the reverse case, where a producer
*wants* the yeast to stop early and uses cold, sulphur, filtration or spirit to
stop it. Reject: any claim of a naturally fermented 25 % drink, and unexamined use
of "wine" for things well above the ceiling.

**Share.** 1.7 % (~2.55 min).

#### 6. The first and biggest question in the taxonomy is whether the sugar was already free

**Claim.** Yeast can only eat simple sugars, so the entire fermented world splits
on whether the raw material offers sugar directly — grapes, honey, cane, apples,
palm sap, milk — or stores its energy as starch, which yeast cannot touch at all;
and that single fact is why grape wine appears wherever grapes grow with almost no
technology, while beer, sake and every grain spirit require an extra industrial
step that had to be invented.

**Why it belongs.** It is the fork the rest of the document is organised on: Act
II is the free-sugar half, Act III is the locked half, and the listener needs the
fork before either. It also explains a real historical pattern for free — the
drinks that need no conversion are older, more widespread and more local, and the
ones that need conversion cluster where cereal agriculture did. Cut it and Acts II
and III are two arbitrary groupings.

**Evidence that counts.** A food scientist, historian of food, or archaeologist
drawing the distinction explicitly: monosaccharides and disaccharides versus
starch as a polymer, why yeast lacks the enzymes, and what that implies about
which drinks can happen accidentally. Strong signal: the accident framing done
honestly — damaged fruit and honey diluted by rain will ferment themselves,
whereas nothing turns a barley field into beer without a human — plus the third
case that beat 44 needs, agave storing fructan rather than starch. Also strong:
archaeological residue evidence with a site and a date attached rather than a
round "9,000 years". Reject: "humans have always fermented things" colour with no
chemistry.

**Share.** 2.0 % (~3.0 min).

#### 7. Malting is tricking the grain into digesting itself

**Claim.** The European and Middle Eastern answer to locked starch is to let the
grain begin to germinate and then stop it — steeping, sprouting, kilning — because
a sprouting seed manufactures the amylase enzymes needed to break its own starch
down, so the maltster is harvesting enzymes rather than flavour; and then mashing
at a chosen temperature between about 63 and 70 °C decides how completely those
enzymes work, which is a direct dial between a thin fermentable wort and a fuller
sweeter one.

**Why it belongs.** It is one of the two great conversion technologies and the
prerequisite for beer, whisky and most grain spirit, which is a large fraction of
everything the listener drinks. It is also the beat where the Foray demonstrates
that a production step can be a *choice with a predictable consequence* rather
than a ritual, and mash temperature is the cleanest example in the subject.
Without it, Act III opens on a process nobody has explained and beat 37 has no
foundation.

**Evidence that counts.** A maltster, brewer or distiller on the mechanics: why
you sprout and why you stop, alpha and beta amylase and the fact that one of them
dies at the temperature the other prefers, the kiln as the flavour step and the
colour range it produces from pale to chocolate to black, diastatic power and why
some malts cannot convert themselves let alone anything else. Strong signal: the
mash-temperature dial explained causally and used to predict a finished drink.
Reject: brewery-tour narration of steps with no mechanism, and "malt" used as a
flavour adjective.

**Share.** 2.0 % (~3.0 min).

#### 8. East Asia's answer was to hire a mould, and it is a better technology than malting

**Claim.** Instead of germinating the grain, the koji and qu traditions grow a
filamentous mould — principally *Aspergillus oryzae* in Japan, a mixed culture in
China and Korea — on cooked grain, and let it secrete the amylases and proteases
from outside; and because the mould keeps converting starch while the yeast is
already fermenting, the sugar arrives gradually rather than all at once, which is
how sake evades the ceiling in beat 5 and reaches strengths no simple mash can.

**Why it belongs.** It is the other half of the world's answer to question one,
and leaving it as a footnote to malting would be both a factual distortion and a
failure of the founder's "all of them". It is also the beat that makes sake,
huangjiu, makgeolli, shochu, soju and baijiu comprehensible as one family later,
which is six drinks explained by one mechanism. And the simultaneity point is
genuinely the most elegant piece of process engineering in the subject.

**Evidence that counts.** A sake brewer, koji specialist, food microbiologist or
fermentation writer on the organism and its enzymes: how koji is propagated on
steamed rice over roughly two days, what proteases contribute that amylases do
not, the distinction between Japanese single-organism koji and Chinese and Korean
mixed starters that carry yeasts and lactic bacteria along with the moulds, and
why parallel conversion and fermentation raises the achievable strength. Strong
signal: *A. oryzae* named as a domesticated relative of a toxin-producing wild
species, and what domestication removed. Reject: koji as a chef's umami
ingredient with no mention of starch, which is a real and different subject.

**Share.** 1.6 % (~2.4 min).

#### 9. There are three more ways to unlock starch, and each of them built a real drink tradition

**Claim.** Malting and moulds are not the only routes: human saliva contains the
same amylase and chewing grain was a working industrial process for chicha and
several Amazonian and Pacific brews; heat alone will hydrolyse agave's fructans,
which is why tequila and mezcal begin in an oven rather than with an enzyme; and
industrial producers today simply buy purified enzymes or use acid, which is how
most of the world's cheap spirit and adjunct beer is actually made.

**Why it belongs.** It completes question one, and completeness here is what makes
the classification key at beat 16 trustworthy — a key with three routes in it
fails on the first Andean or Mexican drink a listener names. It also pre-loads two
later beats with their mechanism already established: beat 34's chewed brews and
beat 44's agave ovens, which is the actual difference between tequila and mezcal.
Cut it and both of those beats have to stop and explain chemistry mid-family.

**Evidence that counts.** A food scientist, anthropologist or producer on any of
the three: salivary amylase and what chewing accomplishes, treated as process
rather than as a shock fact; thermal hydrolysis of inulin and fructans and why
cooking agave for hours or days is a conversion step and not just softening; and
exogenous enzymes and acid hydrolysis in industrial fermentation, stated plainly
rather than as a scandal. Any one of the three advances the beat; two is strong.
Reject: "they chew it, isn't that gross" with no enzymology, and any tape treating
industrial enzymes purely as an adulteration story.

**Share.** 1.3 % (~1.95 min).

#### 10. The flavour is in the by-products, and the producer's real lever is stress

**Claim.** Ethanol and CO2 are nearly all of what fermentation makes and almost
none of what it tastes like — the character comes from a few hundred trace
compounds the yeast makes as side reactions: fruity esters, banana and clove
phenols, buttery diacetyl, higher fusel alcohols, sulphur compounds — and the
producer controls them almost entirely by controlling the yeast's working
conditions, above all temperature, so the same wort or must fermented warm and
fermented cool becomes two different drinks.

**Why it belongs.** It is the answer to "why does it taste of anything", and it is
what lets the listener predict flavour from process for the rest of the Foray:
warm ale is fruity, cold lager is clean, an open wild ferment is sour and funky,
and none of that has to be memorised once the mechanism is held. It also converts
the tasting vocabulary the subject is drowning in into something mechanical, which
is the register this Foray wants. Beat 30 and beat 43 both depend on it.

**Evidence that counts.** A brewer, winemaker, distiller or flavour chemist naming
compounds and their causes: isoamyl acetate and banana, 4-vinyl guaiacol and
clove, diacetyl and what conditioning does to it, fusel alcohols and their link to
temperature and nutrient stress, hydrogen sulphide and its usual fix. Strong
signal: a controlled comparison — the same fermentable run at two temperatures or
with two strains — and an explicit statement that pitching rate, nutrient level and
oxygen are levers too. Reject: pure sensory description, and any tape where the
flavour comes from an ingredient rather than from the ferment.

**Share.** 1.6 % (~2.4 min).

#### 11. Distillation concentrates what is already there, and it cannot go all the way

**Claim.** A still separates rather than creates: ethanol boils at about 78 °C and
water at 100, so the vapour above a heated wash is richer in alcohol than the
liquid, and repeating that enrichment is all distillation is — but the enrichment
runs out, because at roughly 95 to 96 % ethanol the mixture boils as though it were
a single substance and no amount of ordinary distilling will pass that point.

**Why it belongs.** It is the mechanism of the third question, and stating it as
separation rather than as transformation kills the single most common
misconception in the subject: that distillation makes alcohol. It also makes the
azeotrope a fact the listener owns, which pays off immediately at beat 41 — the
reason "pure" vodka is not chemically pure, and the reason fuel ethanol needs a
different technology entirely. And the whole logic of beat 12's column is
unintelligible without the idea of repeated enrichment.

**Evidence that counts.** A distiller, chemical engineer or chemist working the
physics: boiling points, the fact that what matters is vapour composition rather
than one liquid boiling and the other not, why a single pass gives a modest
increase and repeated passes give more, the constant-boiling mixture and its
approximate composition, and what has to be done — drying agents, molecular
sieves, azeotropic distillation — to get past it. Strong signal: a correction of
the "the alcohol boils off first" picture, since that picture is wrong in a way
that matters. Reject: still tours, copper mysticism, and any explanation that has
alcohol being created.

**Share.** 1.8 % (~2.7 min).

#### 12. Two still designs divide the spirits world, and the second one industrialised it

**Claim.** A pot still is a batch device that gives a small number of enrichment
stages, so it carries much of the wash's character forward; a continuous column
gives dozens of stages in one pass and can be run to near-neutral spirit; and the
column's arrival — Cellier-Blumenthal's 1813 patent, Robert Stein's and Aeneas
Coffey's designs of 1826 and 1830 — is the hinge on which vodka, gin, blended
whisky, light rum and industrial spirit all turn, which makes it the most
consequential machine in the subject.

**Why it belongs.** It is the second of Act I's history-is-mechanism beats and it
is the one that most repays being placed before the families: once the listener
knows that a column strips character and a pot preserves it, half of Act IV can be
predicted rather than narrated, and the difference between a single malt and a
blend, or between Jamaican and Cuban rum, becomes obvious instead of tribal. Cut
it and every spirit family has to re-explain its own still.

**Evidence that counts.** A distiller or engineer on the two designs and the
trade: reflux and how still shape and the lyne arm control it, plates or trays as
repeated condensation and re-evaporation, batch versus continuous operation and
what continuity does to labour and cost, and what a column can be tuned to leave
in. Strong signal: dates and attribution for the column with the patents named,
plus the plain economic consequence — cheap neutral spirit as a new industrial
input. Also strong: hybrid stills and why a producer would choose one.
Reject: brand-led still tours, and "handcrafted copper" claims with no account of
reflux.

**Share.** 2.0 % (~3.0 min).

#### 13. Cuts are a real safety operation, and almost everything the public believes about them is misplaced

**Claim.** A distiller discards the first and last fractions of a run because the
volatile compounds are not evenly distributed — acetone, acetaldehyde and methanol
weighted towards the heads, fusel alcohols and fatty acids towards the tails — but
the crucial and widely misunderstood fact is that distillation *redistributes*
methanol rather than removing it, that how much there is to begin with depends on
the feedstock's pectin, which is why fruit and pomace washes are the risky ones
and grain washes are not, and that the mass poisonings that make the news are
almost always industrial methanol added to a drink rather than a badly run still.

**Why it belongs.** It is the beat that makes the Foray trustworthy on a subject
where the folklore is both loud and wrong, and it collects the debt beat 1 opened.
It is also load-bearing for beat 46, where the fruit distillates live and where
the pectin problem is at its worst, and it is what keeps a later mention of
illicit distilling from being a lurid aside — the interesting thing about
moonshine is which risk is real.

**Evidence that counts.** A distiller, chemist or toxicologist on the run profile
and the hazard: what comes over when and why, the practice of cutting by taste,
smell and temperature, pectin methylesterase as the origin of methanol in fruit
washes, and the legal limits that exist per litre of pure alcohol for stone-fruit
spirits specifically. Strong signal: an explicit debunk of "the heads will blind
you if you get it slightly wrong", paired with the accurate version of where
methanol poisoning actually comes from, including the ethanol-as-antidote
mechanism from beat 1. Reject: moonshine folklore repeated straight, and
temperature recipes with no reasoning.

**Share.** 1.4 % (~2.1 min).

#### 14. Maturation is four processes happening at once, and only one of them adds anything

**Claim.** Putting a spirit or wine in a barrel does four distinct things
simultaneously — it *extracts* compounds from the wood, principally vanillin,
lactones, tannins and, if the cask was charred or toasted, caramelised wood
sugars; it *oxidises* the contents slowly through the staves; it *subtracts*,
because char behaves as activated carbon and strips sulphur compounds and
immaturity; and it *concentrates and loses*, because water and ethanol evaporate
at rates that depend on the local climate — so cask size, char level, previous
contents and the warehouse's weather are all production variables, not decoration.

**Why it belongs.** It is question four, and it is the last piece of apparatus the
listener needs. It also does something no other beat can: it makes the difference
between a Scottish and an Indian whisky, between a tawny and a vintage port,
between a first-fill and a refill cask, all instances of one mechanism the
listener already holds. Without it, the entire brown-spirit half of Act IV becomes
a list of house styles. It is also the beat that most reliably corrects the
listener's assumption that older is better.

**Evidence that counts.** A distiller, cooper, cellar master or wood chemist
separating the four mechanisms and ideally quantifying them: what toasting and
charring do differently, oak species and why American and European oak give
different compounds, first-fill versus refill and exhausted casks, surface area to
volume as the real driver of speed, evaporative loss as a percentage per year and
how it differs between a cool damp warehouse and a hot one, and whether the ABV
climbs or falls depending on humidity. Strong signal: the subtractive role of char
stated explicitly, because it is the one usually left out, and an honest statement
that maturation has an optimum after which it degrades. Reject: age-statement
marketing, and "the barrel imparts notes of" with no mechanism.

**Share.** 2.0 % (~3.0 min).

#### 15. Some of the most important drinks in the world are defined by refusing to mature, and other vessels are not neutral either

**Claim.** Maturation is a choice, and a large share of what the world drinks is
built on declining it — vodka, most gin, blanco tequila, the overwhelming majority
of beer, and most white wine are all products whose identity is that nothing was
allowed to happen after fermentation or distillation — while the vessels chosen
instead are themselves process decisions: stainless steel for inertness, buried
clay for the slow micro-oxygenation of Georgian qvevri and Iberian tinajas,
concrete, and the bottle itself, where reductive ageing is a different chemistry
from oxidative ageing in wood.

**Why it belongs.** Without this beat, question four reads as "which wood", and
half the taxonomy is misfiled. It is also the beat that prevents the Foray from
adopting the industry's own prejudice that wood equals quality, and it gives the
listener a real analytic distinction — oxidative versus reductive ageing — that pays
off in beats 53 and 54, where port and sherry are separated by precisely that.

**Evidence that counts.** A winemaker, distiller or brewer arguing for a
non-wood vessel or for no maturation at all, on mechanistic grounds: what
stainless does and does not permit, oxygen transmission through clay and how it
differs from oxygen transmission through oak, why a qvevri buried in the ground is
a temperature and oxygen decision rather than a heritage one, and reductive bottle
ageing as the absence of oxygen rather than the presence of anything. Strong
signal: a producer explaining why they *stopped* using barrels. Reject: natural
wine identity talk with no chemistry, and "unaged means unfinished" assumptions.

**Share.** 1.2 % (~1.8 min).

#### 16. The four questions are a working key, and the listener can now place a drink they have never heard of

**Claim.** The four questions in order — where was the sugar and was it locked,
what converted it and how far did it get, was it concentrated, and what was added
or done afterwards — are sufficient to place every drink in this Foray and every
drink not in it, and the proof is running an unfamiliar one through the key from
scratch and arriving at a correct prediction of its strength, its clarity and
roughly what it tastes like.

**Why it belongs.** This is the beat the Foray exists to deliver and the hinge
into the fan, and it is the difference between "educates me" and "tells me
things". It converts every family that follows from a tour stop into evidence for
a rule the listener already holds, which is the same structural move the barbecue
spine made at its fuel beat, applied to a subject where it matters much more
because the founder asked for completeness. It is also the beat that licenses the
Foray to be short on any given family: a listener with the key does not need every
stop.

**Evidence that counts.** Almost certainly narration, and that is the right
outcome — see §5. If tape is used, the only kind that serves is a speaker
classifying drinks by process across families rather than within one: a drinks
educator or writer who explains that sake is brewed rather than made like wine,
that gin is a flavoured neutral spirit rather than a grain distillate in its own
right, that "rice wine" is a misnomer, and that fortified wine is a wine with a
spirit in it rather than a strong wine. A worked example run out loud on an
unfamiliar drink is the strongest possible tape for this beat and the least likely
to exist. Reject: category listicles, which are the thing this beat replaces.

**Share.** 1.6 % (~2.4 min).

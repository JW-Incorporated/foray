## beat-06 — spine beat 6: The first and biggest question in the taxonomy is whether the sugar was already free

**As spoken.**

> Start with what a yeast can actually eat. Yeast ferments simple sugars — glucose, fructose, and the sucrose it splits into those two. Starch is not one of them. It is a carbohydrate built from glucose units joined end to end in long chains. An enzyme called amylase has to cut those chains into sugars before a yeast can touch them. So the fermented world splits on one question, and it is the first question here. The question is whether the raw material offered its sugar already free, or stored it as starch. Grapes offer it free, as glucose and fructose, once the fruit ripens. Honey is mostly those same two sugars. Cane stores sucrose. Palm sap runs sweet before anything ferments it. Milk sits on the free-sugar side too, though its sugar is one most brewing yeast cannot grow on. Barley, wheat and rye do not offer it free. Neither does rice, and neither does maize. Crushed grapes will ferment on their own, because the yeast is already on the skins and the sugar is already sugar. A barley field doesn't become beer without a person doing something specific to it first. That asymmetry is the fork this whole subject is organised on. The step that unlocks the locked half is called conversion, and there are four ways to do it.

## beat-07 — spine beat 7: Malting is tricking the grain into digesting itself

**As spoken.**

> The European and Middle Eastern answer uses the grain's own machinery. A resting barley grain already holds one starch-cutting enzyme, beta-amylase, in an inactive form. Germination wakes that one and produces the enzymes that will break the grain's starch down. Steep the barley. Let it sprout until the enzymes peak. Then kiln it, which dries the grain and halts the germination. The maltster's whole trick is to allow the sprouting and then stop it. What the maltster is harvesting is enzymes, not flavour. Flavour and colour arrive in the kiln instead, through the Maillard reaction. Then a brewer or a distiller picks a mash temperature, and that choice is a dial with a predictable output. Alpha-amylase peaks between sixty-three and seventy degrees Celsius. Beta-amylase peaks lower, between fifty-five and sixty-five. The two bands nearly overlap, so a single rest usually runs both enzymes at once. Where inside that window the rest sits decides how much of the sugar the yeast can eat. Rest hot and alpha-amylase leaves more sugar the yeast cannot use, so the drink finishes fuller and sweeter. Rest cool instead and the same grain finishes thinner and drier. That is a production step behaving like a dial rather than a ritual.

## beat-09 — spine beat 9: There are three more ways to unlock starch, and each of them built a real drink tradition

**As spoken.**

> Two more routes, and both built real traditions. Human saliva carries an amylase of its own, and it works on starch the same way. Starchy food can taste sweet as it is chewed, because that amylase is already breaking some of it down. For chicha, the maker grinds the maize, moistens it in the mouth, rolls it into balls and dries them. The enzyme in the saliva breaks the maize starch down to maltose. For cassava, the maker chews the root and spits the juice into a bowl. That saliva converts the starch fast enough for wild yeast to take over. Japan had a chewed sake of its own. It is an industrial process, not a curiosity. Heat is the other route. Agave does not store starch; it stores fructans, and heat alone will break them. Agave hearts go into an oven and bake slowly until the fructans become fructose. So an agave spirit begins in an oven, where a grain spirit begins with an enzyme. The first question is now closed.

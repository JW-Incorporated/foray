# Spine — the history of barbecue (`grilling-history-1`)

The canonical narrative, written **source-blind**. Nothing in `data/` was read to
produce this: not the segment pool, not the ASR manifest, not the catalogue, not
a transcript. Only `#226` and `docs/curation/segment-length-rules.md`.

That constraint is the point, and it is worth stating why in the document itself
so nobody later "improves" the spine by checking it against the tape. The
previous `grilling-history-1` drifted into Welsh bakestones, medieval manners and
18th-century dinner parties because its outline was assembled from the tape that
happened to exist. An outliner who can see the catalogue writes an outline the
catalogue fits, and after that no downstream filter can recover the plot, because
the plot has already been bent. So the spine is written first and blind, and the
tape is scored against it.

**Status:** proposed. This is stage 1 of the four stages in #226. Stage 2 sources
each beat independently against that beat's claim; stage 3 reports coverage;
stage 4 assembles in spine order under `segment-length-rules.md`.

**This spine is expected to have holes.** A beat with no tape is a narration
beat, not a beat to be deleted or substituted for. §5 lists the beats I predict
will be hardest to source — as a *prediction for the coverage report*, not a
filter. Nothing was dropped or shortened on a guess about availability, and
guessing at availability is the contamination this document exists to prevent.

---

## 1. The through-line

"History of barbecue" is a subject. Here is the argument.

> **Barbecue is the trade of time for money: it turns cheap, tough protein into
> abundance using fire, smoke and hours instead of cash. That trade has two
> consequences that repeat everywhere it appears, and the whole history falls out
> of them. Because it takes hours, it cannot be one person's weekday dinner — it
> needs a crowd, so it becomes a social institution. And because those hours are
> hot, skilled and punishing, every barbecue culture has to answer the question
> of *who tends the fire* — which is always, underneath, a question about power.**

Two clauses, and they carry different halves of the document.

**Follow the fire and you get technique.** Combustion, smoke chemistry, collagen,
fuel species, the difference between a skewer and a pit. This is why the
traditions resemble each other: they are independent solutions to the same
physics, and the local variables are fuel, animal and faith.

**Follow the labour and you get history.** Somebody stayed up all night. In the
American case that somebody was, for two centuries, enslaved, and then Black and
poorly paid, and the credit went elsewhere — so the American story is not "and
also there is a difficult chapter about slavery." Enslaved and freed Black
cooks are the **authors** of American barbecue, and the modern era is best
understood as the long re-pricing of their labour: converted into suburban
leisure, then into competitive sport, then into chef prestige, and finally into a
contested heritage claim that the craft revival is still arguing about.

The arc the beats follow:

**fire → preservation → surplus → the world's parallel answers → America's
particular answer and who was made to give it → the re-pricing of that labour**

Two tests this through-line has to keep passing, because they are what the old
outline failed:

- **The relevance test is the beat, not the topic.** A segment is not admitted
  for being about food, history or cooking. It is admitted for advancing the
  claim of the beat it is assigned to (#226, founder rule 4).
- **Length is an output.** The proportions in §3 are pacing guidance for a
  ~2–3 hour Foray. They are not quotas, and a beat that comes in at half its
  share because that is all the good tape there was has not failed.

## 2. Where the story chains, and where it fans

This matters to a listener more than it looks like it should, because a jump
inside a chain and a jump inside a fan feel completely different. A chain break
reads as a gap — the listener notices something missing. A fan break reads as a
change of subject, which is fine, but a fan that is *not signalled* as a fan
reads as the narrative losing its place. That is close to what the founder heard
in the bakestone version.

| Act | beats | structure | what the listener should feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Fire, smoke, mechanism | 1–5 | **chain**, causal | each beat is caused by the one before |
| II — Surplus and power | 6–7 | **chain**, causal | the social consequence of Act I |
| III — The world's parallel answers | 8–15 | **fan**, parallel | deliberate stops on a tour; no beat depends on its neighbour |
| IV — The American case | 16–29 | **chain**, chronological | a single line, 1492 to about 1970 |
| V — The re-pricing | 30–38 | **chain**, chronological | 1945 to now |

Three notes on the seams, all of which are assembly instructions as much as
editorial ones:

- **Act III is a fan and must be announced as one.** Narration entering beat 8
  should say plainly that the next stretch is a tour of traditions that do not
  descend from each other, and that the American thread resumes afterwards. Then
  a fan beat with no tape costs the listener nothing — a missing stop on a tour
  is invisible, where a missing link in a chain is a hole. **This means Act III
  is the safest place in the spine for the coverage report to come back thin, and
  Act IV is the most dangerous.**
- **The fan is ordered by lineage, not geography.** Beats 8–12 are the
  direct-heat skewer family, roughly oldest surviving technology first; beats
  13–15 are the whole-animal communal-fire family. Both orderings are defensible;
  what is not defensible is a random walk around the map, because a listener will
  try to infer a principle from the order and will be misled by an arbitrary one.
- **The fan's exit is engineered.** Beat 15 (jerk) is the last fan stop because
  it belongs to both structures: it is a parallel Caribbean tradition *and* the
  living descendant of the technique the English word came from. It hands
  straight to beat 16. If the fan is reordered, keep jerk last.

Act IV's chain has one internal division worth flagging: beats 25–27 (Texas, the
Carolinas, Kansas City and Memphis) are themselves a small fan inside the chain —
parallel regional divergences, not a sequence — and beat 28 is the beat that
turns them back into an argument. Do not cut beat 28; without it the three region
beats are a listicle.

## 3. The beats

**38 beats.** Each has four fields.

- **Claim** — the specific thing this beat asserts. This is what tape is scored
  against, and it is deliberately a sentence that could be true or false, not a
  topic label. "Early fire" is not a claim. "Cooking outsourced digestion" is.
- **Why it belongs** — what the narrative loses if the beat is cut. Beats that
  could not answer this are in §6, deleted.
- **Evidence that counts** — what a good 90–180 s piece of tape actually contains.
  Concrete enough to score a candidate against. Where a name appears it is an
  example of the *kind* of speaker, not a requirement, and not a claim that such
  tape exists — I cannot know that.
- **Share** — proportion of runtime, with minutes at a 150-minute reference.
  Pacing guidance. Not a quota, and never a reason to admit weak tape.

Shares sum to 100 %. A beat's share is a statement about how much the *argument*
needs, decided before anyone knew what was available.

### Act I — Fire, smoke and the mechanism (chain, 14 %)

The act that earns the word "history" in "history of barbecue." It has to do real
work rather than serve as a warm-up, and it has to end pointed at the fan, which
is why beat 5 exists.

#### 1. Cooking is external digestion, and it changed the animal doing it

**Claim.** Applying fire to meat and starch does part of digestion outside the
body — gelatinising starch, denaturing protein, killing parasites — which raised
the calories available per hour of chewing enough to be visible in human anatomy,
and that is the deep reason a species ended up organised around fires.

**Why it belongs.** It is the foundation of the through-line's first clause: fire
is a *technology for extracting value from food that is otherwise expensive to
eat*. Everything later in the spine — smoking tough cuts, twelve hours on a brisket,
jerk on wood, offal on a skewer — is the same move applied to progressively more
specific problems. Cut this and the Foray opens on a grill instead of on an
argument, and the listener never learns what barbecue is *for*.

**Evidence that counts.** A biological anthropologist or archaeologist making the
causal argument out loud — cooked food, higher energy return, smaller gut and
teeth, bigger brain — and ideally engaging the contest over it: Wrangham's
cooking hypothesis versus the objection that the securely dated hearth record is
much younger than the anatomical changes it is meant to explain. Tape that
*names the disagreement* is better tape than tape that asserts the tidy version,
because the disagreement is what makes this history rather than a fun fact. A
specific site with a date attached — Wonderwerk Cave, Gesher Benot Ya'aqov,
Koobi Fora — is a strong signal. Reject: general "fire was important to humans"
colour with no mechanism and no date.

**Share.** 3.5 % (~5 min).

#### 2. Smoke was preservation for thousands of years before it was flavour

**Claim.** Hanging meat and fish in smoke was a storage technology — drying the
surface while depositing phenols and organic acids that suppress bacteria, mould
and rancidity — and the taste we now buy deliberately is a by-product of not
starving in February.

**Why it belongs.** This is the single most important reframing in the Foray and
the beat most likely to change how a listener hears everything after it. It
converts barbecue from a leisure activity into infrastructure, and it sets up the
irony that structures Act V: the modern craft revival sells, at a premium, the
residue of a subsistence technique. Cut this and the deep history is just "people
liked cooking outside," which is not a claim.

**Evidence that counts.** A food historian or food scientist explaining the
*preservation* function specifically and mechanistically: water activity, the
surface pellicle, the antimicrobial and antioxidant action of wood smoke, why
smoke plus salt plus drying together do what none does alone. Strong signal:
named traditional preserved products used as worked examples — biltong, bacon,
kippers, pemmican, boucan — and an explicit statement of the historical ordering
(necessity first, taste after). Reject: smoke-ring or bark chemistry with no
preservation argument. That is beat 3.

**Share.** 3.5 % (~5 min).

#### 3. Low and slow is a specific chemical bargain, and that is why it takes all night

**Claim.** The reason barbecue takes eight to eighteen hours is that its target
cuts are structurally different from a steak — full of collagen that only
converts to gelatin with prolonged heat well above the temperature at which
muscle protein would otherwise dry out — so the cook is running two clocks at
once, and every technique in the tradition is a way of holding them apart.

**Why it belongs.** Without this the listener has no mechanical account of why
barbecue is *slow*, and slowness is the load-bearing fact of the whole
through-line: it is what forces the crowd (beat 6), the labour (beat 20) and the
overnight shift (beat 38). It also arms the listener to hear later beats as
engineering rather than folklore — the stall, the wrap, the offset firebox, the
water pan are all answers to a stated problem.

**Evidence that counts.** A pitmaster or a food scientist naming the mechanism:
collagen to gelatin, connective tissue, rendering fat, the evaporative stall and
what wrapping does about it, Maillard and the bark, why smoke deposits on a moist
surface. Best case is a practitioner who explains their own technique *causally*
rather than as ritual — "I wrap because the surface has stopped losing water and
the temperature stops climbing" beats "we always wrap at six hours." Reject:
recipes, temperature numbers with no reasoning, gear reviews.

**Share.** 3 % (~4.5 min).

#### 4. Two different technologies share one word, and most arguments about barbecue are really about that

**Claim.** There are two distinct fire cuisines — small cuts fast over direct
coals, and large cuts slow in an enclosed or reflected heat away from the fire —
they solve different problems and mostly arose independently, and the English word
"barbecue" now covers both, which is why Americans, Britons, Australians and
South Africans can all be describing something different in good faith.

**Why it belongs.** This is the spine's organising distinction and the thing that
makes Act III comprehensible. Without it, yakitori and whole hog are just two
items on a list; with it, they are two answers with different logics, and the
listener can predict which one a new tradition will resemble from its fuel and its
animal. It also pre-empts the most tedious possible listener objection — "that
isn't barbecue" — by making the ambiguity a subject rather than an accident.

**Evidence that counts.** Someone drawing the distinction explicitly and
defending it: direct versus indirect, grilling versus barbecue, and the
observation that the usage split follows dialect rather than technique. Useful
variant: a speaker who resists the distinction and argues the word should stay
broad. Either advances the beat; what does not is an unexamined use of the word.
Reject: definitional bickering with no account of the underlying difference in
technique.

**Share.** 2 % (~3 min).

#### 5. Fuel is the independent variable — it explains more of the world map than taste does

**Claim.** Which tree or fuel is abundant locally determines the flavour, the
achievable temperature and therefore the shape of the whole tradition, so the
world's fire cuisines are largely a map of botany and forestry: post oak and
mesquite in Texas, pimento wood in Jamaica, quebracho in Argentina, oak and beech
charcoal in Turkey, binchōtan oak in Japan, vine cuttings and olive prunings
around the Mediterranean, dried dung and scrub where there is no timber at all.

**Why it belongs.** It is the hinge into Act III, and it converts the fan from a
tour into a demonstration. Give the listener this beat and the eight traditions
that follow become evidence for a rule they already hold, which is the difference
between a tour that coheres and a tour that wanders. It also inoculates against
the mystical register that fuel talk usually attracts.

**Evidence that counts.** A pitmaster, charcoal producer or food scientist
explaining what a specific wood does and why — burn temperature, smoke
composition, density, moisture, why some woods are unusable — and ideally
connecting availability to tradition rather than to preference. Tape from *any*
tradition serves this beat, which makes it flexible in sourcing; tape that
compares two fuels is worth more than tape praising one. Reject: brand and
product talk about bagged charcoal, and unsupported flavour poetry.

**Share.** 2 % (~3 min).

### Act II — Surplus and power (chain, 6 %)

Two beats, and they are the pivot from technique to history. If the Foray is
going to earn its second hour, it earns it here — this is where cooking becomes
politics, and every later act depends on it.

#### 6. Cooking a whole animal produces more food than a household can eat, so it requires a crowd

**Claim.** Whole-carcass fire cooking is economically irrational for one family —
too much fuel, too many hours, more meat than can be stored — so wherever it
persists it is embedded in an occasion that assembles people, which makes the pit
a social institution and not only a cooking method.

**Why it belongs.** It is the load-bearing middle term of the through-line: it is
*why* barbecue keeps turning into identity, festival, church supper, election
rally and national holiday, in cultures with no contact with each other. Cut it
and every communal beat later — the braai as a national ritual, the political
barbecue, Juneteenth, the suburban cookout — becomes an unexplained coincidence.

**Evidence that counts.** A historian or anthropologist making the structural
argument: pre-refrigeration meat economics, why slaughter was an event, why the
feast is the storage technology when you cannot preserve everything, the labour
and fuel arithmetic of a whole animal. Also strong: a practitioner describing the
scale plainly — how many people a whole hog feeds, how many hands it takes, what
happens to leftovers. Reject: generic "food brings people together" sentiment
with no economics under it.

**Share.** 3 % (~4.5 min).

#### 7. Feeding a crowd meat from a fire is a claim to authority, and it has been used as one for as long as we have records

**Claim.** Because the pit produces abundance visibly and at cost, whoever
provides it is making a public statement about their capacity and their claim on
the crowd — which is why fire-feasting shows up in sacrifice, patronage,
hospitality obligation and, in the American case, in electioneering so
consistently that "barbecue" became a synonym for a political rally.

**Why it belongs.** This is the beat that makes the second clause of the
through-line — power — arrive before America rather than looking like a
grievance imported into it. It also means beat 22 lands as a continuation rather
than a surprise, and it gives the listener a lens they will apply themselves to
the braai and the asado without being told.

**Evidence that counts.** A historian on the politics of feeding: sacrifice and
distribution in the ancient Mediterranean or Near East, the obligations of
hospitality, aristocratic patronage through meat, or directly on American
campaign barbecues as a machine for assembling voters. The claim to look for is
*reciprocity or obligation* — the crowd owes something for the meat. Reject:
food-history colour about big historical feasts with no argument about power.
This is exactly the boundary the bakestone drift crossed: a lavish dinner party
is not this beat.

**Share.** 3 % (~4.5 min).

### Act III — The world's parallel answers (fan, 24 %)

Eight traditions that did not descend from each other. They are here because the
founder required world coverage, and they earn their place structurally: they are
the evidence for beats 4, 5 and 6. Each is an independent solution to the same
physics under different constraints of fuel, animal, wealth and religion, and
watching the constraint change while the problem stays fixed is the pleasure of
this act.

**Beats 8–12 are the direct-heat skewer family; 13–15 are the whole-animal
communal-fire family.** Sourcing must not quietly reorder them into geographic
sequence — see §2.

English-language sourcing only, per #226. That is a real constraint here and it
biases what is findable, but it does not license shrinking the act: an English
food historian or a diaspora practitioner speaking English on any of these
traditions serves the beat fully. What it does mean is that some of these beats
will likely be narration, and §5 says which.

#### 8. On the steppe, scarcity of fuel — not abundance — shaped the technique, and the thing sold as "Mongolian barbecue" is not it

**Claim.** Central Asian and Mongolian herding cultures cooked mutton and goat
with almost no timber, which pushed them toward boiling, steam and hot-stone
methods — meat cooked inside the animal or in a sealed vessel with fire-heated
rocks — while the skewered-over-coals tradition belongs more to the settled and
Caucasus edges of that world; and the restaurant format Westerners call Mongolian
barbecue is a mid-20th-century Taipei invention with no steppe ancestry.

**Why it belongs.** It is the fan's sharpest test of beat 5. Every other stop on
the tour has fuel; this one does not, and the technique diverges exactly as the
rule predicts. It also lets the Foray correct a widely believed error, which is
one of the few genuinely delightful things a history can do. Cut it and the fan
loses its control case — the tradition where the answer is *not* a grill.

**Evidence that counts.** A food historian or anthropologist on steppe pastoral
cooking: khorkhog and boodog and how hot-stone cooking works, dung and scrub as
fuel, why the cauldron dominates, what mobility does to a kitchen. Separately
strong: a clean debunk of the Mongolian-barbecue restaurant's origin, ideally
naming the Taipei provenance. Reject: Genghis-Khan-cooked-under-his-saddle
folklore repeated straight, and any tape whose only content is a stir-fry buffet.

**Share.** 2.5 % (~3.75 min).

#### 9. The skewer over coals is the oldest grill technology still in daily use, and the Ottoman world industrialised it into a cuisine

**Claim.** Meat on a metal or wooden skewer over a long narrow bed of charcoal —
the mangal — is an unbroken technique with material evidence in the Bronze Age
Aegean, and the Ottoman and wider Middle Eastern world developed it into a
codified professional cuisine of regional kebabs, out of which even the modern
global street food of vertical döner is a recent and traceable invention.

**Why it belongs.** It is the origin point of the direct-heat family and the
tradition with the best claim to continuity of any in the Foray. It also carries
the fan's clearest case of a technique that stayed the same while empires changed
around it, and its diaspora — Balkans to Berlin to every high street — is the one
world tradition the Anglophone listener has almost certainly eaten. Cut it and the
skewer family has no root.

**Evidence that counts.** A food historian on kebab as a professional cuisine:
the mangal as equipment and as a social occasion, regional codification of named
kebabs, the butchery and mincing a kebab actually requires, charcoal management
along a narrow fire. Also strong: the documented 19th-century turn of the spit
from horizontal to vertical, and its 1970s German reinvention as a sandwich.
Archaeological tape on Bronze Age skewer supports is a bonus, not the beat.
Reject: restaurant-scene reportage with no history in it.

**Share.** 3 % (~4.5 min).

#### 10. Radiant clay and the skewer economy: South and Southeast Asia solved the same problem with an oven and with street-scale portions

**Claim.** The tandoor turns the fire into a radiant enclosure rather than a bed
of coals — a third physics, distinct from both of beat 4's families — and
Southeast Asia's satay tradition pushed the skewer to its economic extreme, tiny
portions cooked to order by mobile vendors, so that in both cases the technology
follows the unit of sale as much as the food.

**Why it belongs.** Without it the fan omits South and Southeast Asia entirely,
which a well-read listener will notice, and it adds a genuine third case to beat
4's pair: not direct coals and not an offset pit but radiant walls. It is also the
cleanest illustration that portion size is a technological input — a satay stick
exists because of what a customer can afford to buy at once.

**Evidence that counts.** A food historian on the tandoor: how a clay oven's heat
differs from a grill's, its distribution across Central and South Asia, and the
well-documented mid-20th-century invention of tandoori chicken and its onward
path into Britain. On satay: Javanese origins, the possible kebab influence, and
street-vending economics — who cooks it, how it is sold, what a portion costs.
Reject: recipe and menu material, and curry-house history with no fire in it.

**Share.** 2 % (~3 min).

#### 11. Yakitori is a thrift cuisine turned into a precision craft by its fuel

**Claim.** Grilling skewered chicken — including the parts nobody else wanted —
became a Japanese urban staple only after centuries of religiously grounded
prohibitions on eating meat had lapsed, and its distinction lies less in the
recipe than in fuel and control: dense white charcoal that runs very hot with very
little smoke, which makes exactness possible and leaves the cook's judgement as
the whole product.

**Why it belongs.** It is the strongest counterexample in the Foray to the
assumption that fire cooking means smoke and volume. Here the tradition
deliberately engineers smoke *out*, works in grams rather than kilos, and arrives
at prestige from thrift — which is the same arc Act V describes for brisket, a
century or more earlier and on the other side of the world. That parallel is worth
the runtime on its own.

**Evidence that counts.** A food historian or a chef explaining: the meat
prohibitions and their Meiji-era lifting as the precondition, the postwar street
stall and its place in urban working life, binchōtan and why hard high-heat
low-smoke charcoal changes what is possible, whole-bird thrift and the named
cuts, tare versus salt. Best case is a practitioner articulating why the fuel
dictates the technique — that ties the beat back to beat 5. Reject: izakaya
travelogue.

**Share.** 3 % (~4.5 min).

#### 12. Korea's move was to put the fire on the table and hand the cooking to the guest

**Claim.** Korean grilled meat is structurally unusual because the diner is the
cook — the heat sits in the middle of the table and the meal is assembled in the
hand from grilled meat plus wrappers and condiments — so the tradition's
distinctive content is a social arrangement rather than a technique, and its most
famous forms are far more recent than they feel.

**Why it belongs.** It is the fan's clearest demonstration of beat 6 running in
reverse: instead of a crowd assembling because of a long cook, the cooking itself
is divided among the crowd. It also lets the Foray make an honest point about
invented tradition — the marinated court-cuisine lineage is genuinely old, but the
pork-belly restaurant culture that now defines the category is a 20th-century
development with an economic explanation. That is the same historiographic muscle
beats 25–27 and 36 need.

**Evidence that counts.** A food historian on gogigui: the older marinated
sliced-beef lineage and its court associations, the mid-century arrival of
table-top grilling equipment, the specific and recent rise of pork belly and the
meat economics behind it, ssam as a technique, charcoal versus gas in restaurants
and what that changed. Also good: a Korean or Korean-American chef or writer on
how the form travelled and what got fixed in translation. Reject: menu explainer.

**Share.** 3 % (~4.5 min).

#### 13. The braai is the case where a country asked its fire to do political work

**Claim.** In South Africa the fire is not only a way to cook but an explicitly
claimed national ritual, with its own public-holiday campaign — and that claim is
made across a society that was legally segregated within living memory, so the
braai is simultaneously a genuinely shared practice, a set of parallel traditions
with different names and histories, and an argument about who "we" are.

**Why it belongs.** It is the through-line's second clause stated outside the
American act, and it is the proof that the American story is not unique in binding
fire to identity and to race. Placing it here means Act IV arrives as an instance
of a pattern rather than as an exception, which is both better history and better
narrative. Cut it and the American reckoning looks parochial.

**Evidence that counts.** A South African historian, food writer or practitioner
on: braai as national ritual and the Heritage Day campaign, and honestly on the
contest around it; the distinct lineages sitting under the one word, including
shisa nyama and township grill culture, and sosaties as a Cape Malay inheritance
carried by enslaved people from the Indian Ocean world; boerewors and its
butchery. The claim to look for is fire-as-identity being asserted *and*
contested. Reject: tourism-board material, and "South Africans love to braai"
with nothing under it.

**Share.** 3.5 % (~5.25 min).

#### 14. In Argentina, meat was so cheap and wood so plentiful that the whole craft moved into time and fire management

**Claim.** The pampas produced beef in quantities almost nowhere else could match,
which removed scarcity as a constraint and left the asado's entire skill
concentrated in fuel, distance and patience — and it formalised the person doing
it into a social office, the asador, whose authority over the fire and the carving
is a recognised role rather than a chore.

**Why it belongs.** It is the fan's inverse of beat 8 — abundance instead of
scarcity — and together the two bracket beat 5's rule. It is also the tradition
that most cleanly shows the "who tends the fire" question being answered *with
prestige*, which is exactly the contrast beat 20 needs in order to land: the same
job, honoured here and coerced there.

**Evidence that counts.** A historian or an asador on: the origins of pampas
cattle abundance and its 19th-century export economy, gaucho fire cooking, the
parrilla and the whole-animal cross, hardwood charcoal and coal management,
ordering and timing across offal and cuts, and the role and authority of the
asador. Best case explains *why* an Argentine cook thinks about fire rather than
about seasoning. Reject: steakhouse boosterism, and anything whose only content is
that Argentines eat a lot of beef.

**Share.** 3.5 % (~5.25 min).

#### 15. Jerk is the living Caribbean descendant of the technique the English word came from, and it was refined by people who had escaped slavery

**Claim.** Jamaican jerk descends from the indigenous Caribbean pit-and-frame
method the Spanish transliterated as *barbacoa*, developed under the specific
conditions of Maroon communities of formerly enslaved Africans living
independently in the interior — where a low pit burning aromatic local wood cooked
tough meat slowly while giving off comparatively little visible smoke — and it
became a commercial roadside cuisine only in the twentieth century.

**Why it belongs.** Two jobs, which is why it closes the fan. It is a full
tradition in its own right, and it is the bridge: the same technique lineage Act
IV picks up at beat 16, carried by the same collision of indigenous technique,
African cooks and enslavement that produced American barbecue, reached from a
different direction. Cut it and the fan-to-chain seam becomes an abrupt subject
change instead of a handoff.

**Evidence that counts.** A Caribbean food historian or a jerk practitioner on:
Taíno technique and the barbacoa frame, Maroon history and why concealment and
long slow cooking suited it, pimento wood and allspice and Scotch bonnet as local
inputs rather than as a recipe, the 20th-century move to oil-drum pans and
roadside commerce, and the diaspora spread. The claim to look for is the
*continuity* argument — that this is the old technique still being practised — and
the political condition it developed under. Reject: recipe and heat-level content,
and food-travel segments with no history.

**Share.** 3.5 % (~5.25 min).
